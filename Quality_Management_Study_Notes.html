Section 01

Software Quality — Definitions & Dimensions

Core Definition

Software Quality is defined in terms of its fitness of purpose — a quality product does precisely what the users want it to do, meeting all requirements laid down in the SRS document.

However, "fitness of purpose" alone is not sufficient for software. A product may be functionally correct yet still fail as a quality product if it has a poor user interface, excessive bugs, or is impossible to maintain.

Illustrative Example

Consider software that performs all tasks specified in the SRS but has an almost unusable user interface. Even though it is functionally correct, it cannot be considered a quality product. This shows why modern quality goes beyond mere correctness.

Section 02

Quality Management System & Evolution

Definition

A Quality Management System (QMS) is the principal method used by organizations to ensure that products they develop have the desired quality.

Components of a QMS

  • Managerial Structure & Individual Responsibilities: Quality is an organization-wide responsibility. A dedicated quality department exists, and support from top management is essential — without it, staff won't take quality seriously.
  • Quality System Activities: Includes auditing of projects, review of the QMS, development of standards/methods/guidelines, and production of effectiveness reports for top management.

Evolution of Quality Management

Quality systems have evolved through four stages over the last five decades:

StageNameFocus
1stProduct InspectionInspect finished products; remove defective ones. No root-cause analysis.
2ndQuality Control (QC)Detect defects AND determine causes behind them. Corrects root causes, not just outcomes.
3rdQuality Assurance (QA)"If processes are proper and rigorously followed, products will be of good quality." Focus shifts to process.
4thTotal Quality Management (TQM)Continuously improve processes through measurement. Goes beyond documenting to optimizing (reengineering).
Key Insight

The quality paradigm has shifted from product assurance → process assurance. TQM is linked to Business Process Reengineering (BPR), which re-engineers how business is carried out in an organization.

Model Overview

McCall's Model categorizes software quality factors into three perspectives: Product Operation, Product Revision, and Product Transition.

🖥️

Product Operation

Correctness, Reliability, Efficiency, Integrity, Usability — how well the software runs day-to-day.

🔧

Product Revision

Maintainability, Flexibility, Testability — how easily the software can be changed or tested.

🚀

Product Transition

Portability, Reusability, Interoperability — how well the software moves to new environments or integrates with others.

Section 04

Software Reliability

Definition 1

Software Reliability (Operational Reliability) is the ability of a system or component to perform its required functions under stated conditions for a specified period of time.

Definition 2 (Probabilistic)

The probability that a software system fulfills its assigned task in a given environment for a predefined number of input cases, assuming hardware and input are error-free.

Key Characteristics

  • An essential component of software quality, alongside functionality, usability, performance, and maintainability.
  • Hard to achieve because software complexity is high.
  • Unlike hardware, software has no physical wear — it fails due to design/specification errors.
  • Complexity is inversely proportional to reliability but directly proportional to functionality.
Real-world Scale Example

Next-gen aircraft: over 1 million source lines of software (SLOC). Air Traffic Control: 1–2 million lines. International Space Station: 2 million on-board + 10 million ground support. Defense systems: 5+ million SLOC. These illustrate why reliability is mission-critical.

Reliability Metrics & Numericals

MetricFormulaPurpose
MTBFTotal Operating Time ÷ Number of FailuresAverage time between consecutive failures
MTTFTotal Operating Time ÷ Total FailuresMean time to first failure (new systems)
MTTRTotal Repair Time ÷ Number of RepairsAverage time to repair and restore system
AvailabilityMTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR)System uptime percentage
Failure Rate (λ)Number of Failures ÷ Operating TimeFrequency of failures per unit time
📐 Numerical Problem 1

Problem: A software system operates for 1000 hours and experiences 5 failures. Total repair time is 10 hours. Calculate MTBF, MTTF, MTTR, Availability, and Failure Rate.


MTTF = 1000 / 5 = 200 hours

MTTR = 10 / 5 = 2 hours

MTBF = MTTF + MTTR = 200 + 2 = 202 hours

Availability = 202 / (202 + 2) = 202/204 ≈ 0.9902 = 99.02%

Failure Rate λ = 5 / 1000 = 0.005 failures/hour
📐 Numerical Problem 2

Problem: A system has MTBF = 500 hours and MTTR = 5 hours. Find availability and failure rate.


Availability = 500 / (500 + 5) = 500/505 ≈ 0.9901 = 99.01%

Failure Rate = 1 / MTBF = 1/500 = 0.002 failures/hour
📐 Numerical Problem 3 — High Availability Requirement

Problem: A safety-critical system must have 99.9% availability. If MTTR = 1 hour, what is the minimum required MTBF?


Let MTBF = x. Then: x / (x + 1) ≥ 0.999

x ≥ 0.999x + 0.999
0.001x ≥ 0.999
x ≥ 999 hours

Therefore, the system must have MTBF ≥ 999 hours to meet the 99.9% availability requirement.

Section 07

Software Reviews

Definition

Software Review is a systematic inspection of software by one or more individuals working together to find and resolve errors and defects during the early stages of the SDLC.

Objectives of Software Review

  • Improve productivity of the development team
  • Make testing time- and cost-effective
  • Deliver final software with fewer defects
  • Eliminate inadequacies in the software

Types of Software Reviews

1. Software Peer Review

Assesses technical content and quality; conducted by the author along with developers. Sub-types:

Sub-typeDescription
Code ReviewSystematic examination of source code for defects
Pair ProgrammingTwo developers code together on the same platform simultaneously
WalkthroughAuthor guides team; participants ask questions and comment on defects
Technical ReviewHighly qualified team examines product against client use & standards
InspectionReviewers follow a well-defined process systematically to find defects

2. Software Management Review

Evaluates work status. Decisions regarding downstream activities are taken based on the findings.

3. Software Audit Review

External review by critics not part of the development team. They conduct an independent inspection to assess compliance with specifications and standards. Performed by managerial-level personnel.

Advantages of Software Reviews

  • Defects identified at an earlier stage of development (especially in formal reviews)
  • Earlier inspection reduces maintenance cost significantly
  • Can be used to train technical authors
  • Helps remove process inadequacies that encourage defects
Section 08

Formal Technical Review (FTR)

Definition

Formal Technical Review (FTR) is a software quality control activity performed by software engineers. It includes walkthroughs, inspections, round-robin reviews, and other small-group technical assessments conducted as structured meetings.

Objectives of FTR

  • Uncover errors in logic, function, and implementation
  • Verify software meets specified requirements
  • Ensure software follows predefined standards
  • Promote uniform development style
  • Make the project more manageable
  • Enable junior engineers to observe approach of senior engineers

FTR Roles & Responsibilities

RoleResponsibilities
ModeratorPlans, facilitates, and controls the entire review meeting process
AuthorCreates the work product; explains technical details to reviewers
ReviewerExamines product carefully; identifies defects and suggests improvements
RecorderDocuments all issues, defects, and action items during the meeting
ManagerEnsures adequate resources are available; has no evaluation role

The Review Meeting — Constraints

  • Between 3–5 people should be involved
  • Advance preparation: at most 2 hours per person
  • Meeting duration: less than 2 hours
  • Focuses on a specific and small part of the overall software

Meeting Outcomes (Decision)

  • Accept without any modification
  • Reject due to serious error (another review required after correction)
  • Conditional Accept — minor errors to be corrected, no additional review needed

FTR Guidelines

  • Review the product, not the producer
  • Take written notes
  • Limit number of participants; insist on advance preparation
  • Develop a checklist for each product
  • Set an agenda and maintain it
  • Separate problem areas but don't attempt to solve every problem noted during the meeting
  • Limit debate and rebuttal

Software Quality Assurance (SQA)

Definition

Software Quality Assurance (SQA) is a set of activities for ensuring quality in software engineering processes. It ensures that developed software meets and complies with defined or standardized quality specifications throughout the SDLC.

SQA is an ongoing process — unlike testing which checks quality after coding, SQA checks for quality in each phase of development.

SQA Activities Include:

  • Process definition and implementation
  • Auditing
  • Training

SQA Responsibilities

  • Identify weaknesses in processes
  • Correct those weaknesses to continually improve

  • Review of project schedule and resource estimates
  • Evaluation of customer's capacity to fulfil obligations
  • Evaluation of development risks

  • Criteria for starting and ending each project stage
  • Lists of reviews, tests, and other scheduled verification & validation activities

Statistical SQA

Definition

Statistical SQA uses quantitative and statistical methods to measure and control software quality. It enables data-driven decision-making and provides objective evidence of achieved quality levels.

Statistical Techniques

TechniqueApplicationPurpose
Pareto Analysis (80-20 Rule)Defect categorizationIdentify the ~20% causes responsible for ~80% of defects
Control Charts (SPC)Process monitoringDetect process variations; identify out-of-control conditions
HistogramData distributionVisualize frequency patterns across different data ranges
Scatter DiagramCorrelation analysisIdentify relationships between two quality-related variables
Run ChartsTrend analysisMonitor changes over time; identify emerging trends early
Fishbone (Ishikawa) DiagramRoot cause analysisIdentify root causes of defects (used at CMM Level 4)

Statistical Process Control (SPC)

  • Control Limits: Define acceptable process variation using capability baselines (UCL = Upper, LCL = Lower, CL = Center Line)
  • Charts distinguish common cause variation from special cause variation
  • Corrective actions trigger when processes exceed defined limits
  • Goal: maintain consistent long-term process stability
Pareto Example

After analyzing 100 bugs in a software project: Requirement errors = 40, Design errors = 25, Coding errors = 20, Testing gaps = 15. A Pareto chart reveals that requirement and design errors (65%) are the vital few to address first for maximum defect reduction.

ISO 9000 Certification

Definition

ISO (International Standards Organization) is a consortium of 63 countries established to promote standardization. The ISO 9000 series, declared in 1987, determines guidelines for maintaining a quality system — focusing on organizational and operational methods.

Key premise: "If a proper process is followed for production, good quality products will automatically follow."

ISO 9003Only installation and testing — e.g., gas companies❌ Limited applicability

ISO 9000 Certification Process

  1. Application: Organization applies to the registrar for registration
  2. Pre-Assessment: Registrar makes a rough assessment of the organization
  3. Document Review & Adequacy Audit: Registrar reviews documents and suggests improvements
  4. Compliance Audit: Registrar checks if suggestions have been implemented
  5. Registration: ISO certification awarded after all phases pass
  6. Continued Inspection: Registrar monitors the organization periodically

Section 12

SEI-CMM Framework

Definition

The Capability Maturity Model (CMM), developed by the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) at Carnegie Mellon University, is a framework used to analyze approaches and techniques followed by organizations to develop software, and to provide guidelines for enhancing process maturity.

CMM is NOT a software process model — it is a framework for process assessment and improvement. It defines 5 levels of process maturity. All levels except Level 1 have Key Process Areas (KPAs).

The 5 Maturity Levels

1
Initial (Chaotic)
Ad hoc, unpredictable processes. No defined process. Different engineers follow different methods. Success depends on individual heroics. Also called the "chaotic level." No KPAs.
2
Repeatable
Basic project management practices established. Cost and schedule tracking implemented. Size & cost estimation methods used (Function Point Analysis, COCOMO, etc.). Results are repeatable across similar projects.
3
Defined
Both management and development processes are defined, documented, and standardized organization-wide. Common understanding of roles and responsibilities. ISO 9000 certification targets this level. Process and product quality not yet measured quantitatively.
4
Managed
Focus on software metrics. Product metrics (size, reliability, complexity) and Process metrics (defect correction time, productivity, defects per LOC) are collected. Tools: Pareto charts, fishbone diagrams. Quantitative quality requirements are met. Metrics used to evaluate project performance.
5
Optimizing
Continuous process improvement based on process and product measurement data. Defect prevention. Technology change management. Process change management. Innovation-driven quality improvement.

Key Process Areas (KPAs) by Level

LevelKey Process Areas
Level 2Requirements Management, Project Planning, Tracking & Oversight, Subcontract Management, Software Quality Assurance, Configuration Management
Level 3Org. Process Focus, Org. Process Definition, Training Program, Integrated Software Management, Software Product Engineering, Intergroup Coordination, Peer Reviews
Level 4Quantitative Process Management, Software Quality Management
Level 5Defect Prevention, Technology Change Management, Process Change Management
Key Relationship

Each CMM level builds on the previous. An organization cannot skip levels. ISO 9000 targets Level 3 (Defined). CMM provides a roadmap from Level 1 to Level 5 through gradual, step-by-step process improvement.

Q02

2 marks

MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures) is the average time elapsed between consecutive failures in a repairable system. It is a key reliability metric.

Formula: MTBF = Total Operating Time ÷ Number of Failures. A higher MTBF indicates a more reliable system.

Q04
What does CMM stand for, and who developed it?

Q05
What is software portability?
2 marks

Portability is a software quality attribute that indicates how freely a software product can be made to work in various operating system environments, on multiple machines, and with other software products. A highly portable program (e.g., a Java application) requires minimal modification when moved to a different platform.

Q06
What is software availability? Give its formula.
2 marks

Availability measures the percentage of time a system is operational and accessible to users. It is calculated as:

Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)

For example, if MTBF = 100 hours and MTTR = 1 hour, Availability = 100/101 ≈ 99.01%.

It applies to software development organizations because they design and develop software products. It is the most relevant ISO 9000 series standard for the software industry.

It is primarily educational in purpose, helping team members understand the code/design and identify issues collaboratively.

🟡 Medium — 5 Marks (10–15 Lines)
Q11
Explain the evolution of Quality Management Systems through four stages.
5 marks

Quality management systems have evolved through four stages over the last five decades:

Stage 1 — Product Inspection (Pre-WWII): The only quality function was to inspect finished products and remove defective ones. There was no analysis of why defects occurred — just binary accept/reject.

Stage 2 — Quality Control (QC): QC went beyond detection to determining the root causes of defects. The goal became correcting the reasons for bugs, not just rejecting products. This introduced cause-effect analysis into quality processes.

Stage 3 — Quality Assurance (QA): The primary premise of QA is that if an organization's processes are properly defined and rigorously followed, products will be of good quality. QA focuses on process definition, process analysis, and process improvement rather than end-product inspection. ISO 9000 embodies this philosophy.

Stage 4 — Total Quality Management (TQM): TQM advocates continuous process improvement through measurement. It goes beyond documenting processes to actively optimizing them through redesign (Business Process Reengineering). TQM closes the loop: measure → analyze → improve → repeat.

The overarching trend is a shift from product assurance to process assurance.

Q12
Describe the five maturity levels of SEI-CMM with their key characteristics.
5 marks

The SEI-CMM defines five maturity levels:

Level 1 — Initial (Chaotic): Processes are ad hoc and undisciplined. No defined process exists. Engineers follow their own methods, making development unpredictable and chaotic. Success depends on individual effort and heroics.

Level 2 — Repeatable: Basic project management practices (cost tracking, schedule tracking) are established. Estimation methods like Function Point Analysis and COCOMO are used. Results are repeatable for similar projects due to disciplined procedures.

Level 3 — Defined: Both management and development processes are defined, documented, and standardized across the organization. Everyone understands their roles and responsibilities. ISO 9000 aims at this level. However, process/product quality is not yet measured quantitatively.

Level 4 — Managed: Software metrics are collected. Product metrics (size, reliability, complexity) and process metrics (defect correction time, productivity, defects per LOC) are used. Quantitative quality requirements are set and met. Tools like Pareto charts and fishbone diagrams are used.

Level 5 — Optimizing: Both process and product metrics drive continuous process improvement. Focus is on defect prevention, technology change management, and process change management. Innovation is used proactively to optimize performance.

Each level builds on the previous, and organizations cannot skip levels. KPAs guide improvement from one level to the next (except Level 1, which has no KPAs).

Q13
What are the objectives, roles, and guidelines of Formal Technical Review (FTR)?
5 marks

Objectives: FTR uncovers errors in logic, function, and implementation; verifies software meets requirements; ensures adherence to standards; promotes uniform development; and makes projects more manageable. It also helps junior engineers learn from experienced ones.

Roles: The Moderator plans, facilitates, and controls the meeting. The Author explains the work product. The Reviewer examines the product and identifies defects. The Recorder documents all issues and action items. The Manager provides resources but doesn't evaluate.

Meeting Constraints: 3–5 participants; advance preparation ≤2 hours per person; meeting ≤2 hours.

Outcomes: Accept without modification; Reject with serious errors (re-review needed); Conditional Accept (minor errors to fix, no re-review).

Key Guidelines: Review the product, not the producer. Take written notes. Set an agenda and maintain it. Develop checklists. Limit debate and rebuttal. Separate problem identification from problem solving.

Q14
Explain the ISO 9000 certification process with all its stages.
5 marks

ISO 9000 certification is obtained through a structured six-stage process:

1. Application: The organization decides to pursue ISO certification and submits an application to the registrar for registration. This is a formal commitment to quality improvement.

2. Pre-Assessment: The registrar conducts a rough, preliminary assessment of the organization to understand its current state and readiness for full audit.

3. Document Review & Adequacy Audit: The registrar reviews all documentation submitted by the organization (quality manual, procedures, policies) and suggests specific improvements needed.

4. Compliance Audit: The registrar checks whether the organization has actually implemented the suggested improvements. This is a thorough on-site assessment.

5. Registration: If the compliance audit is successful, the registrar awards the ISO 9000 certification. The organization is officially registered.

6. Continued Inspection (Surveillance): Certification is not permanent. The registrar conducts periodic follow-up audits to ensure the organization maintains its quality system. This ensures ongoing compliance.

The entire process is based on ISO 9000:2015, which is grounded in seven quality management principles: Customer Focus, Leadership, Engagement of People, Process Approach, Improvement, Evidence-Based Decision Making, and Relationship Management.

Q15
What is Software Reliability? Discuss factors that make it difficult to achieve.
5 marks

Software Reliability is the probability that a software system fulfills its assigned task in a given environment for a predefined number of input cases, assuming hardware and input are error-free. Operationally, it is the ability to perform required functions under stated conditions for a specified time period.

Why it is hard to achieve:

1. High Complexity: Modern software systems contain millions of lines of code, making it impossible to test every possible execution path or input combination. Complexity is inversely proportional to reliability.

2. No Physical Wear: Unlike hardware, software doesn't wear out — but it fails due to design errors or specification mismatches that weren't detected during testing.

3. Scale: Systems like air traffic control (1–2 million SLOC), Space Station software (2M on-board + 10M ground support), and defense systems (5M+ SLOC) have enormous potential defect surfaces.

4. Developer-Driven Complexity Push: System developers tend to push complexity into software layers for flexibility, increasing the reliability challenge.

5. Environment Variability: Software operates in diverse environments with different inputs, user behaviors, and hardware configurations, making exhaustive reliability testing impractical.

Reliability is an essential component of software quality alongside functionality, usability, performance, and maintainability.

Q16
Describe the components of an SQA system with their roles.
5 marks

An SQA system combines six classes of components:

1. Pre-Project Components: Ensure project commitments are clearly defined — resources, schedule, budget, and quality plans are correctly determined before work begins. Includes Contract Review and Development/Quality Plan preparation.

2. Project Life Cycle Assessment Components: Cover both the development stage (reviews, expert opinions, testing to detect design and programming errors) and the operation-maintenance stage (specialized maintenance quality checks).

3. Infrastructure Error Prevention Components: Applied organization-wide to eliminate or reduce error rates based on the organization's accumulated SQA experience. Focuses on systemic root-cause elimination.

4. Software Quality Management Components: Control development and maintenance activities; introduce early managerial support to prevent schedule and budget failures and their consequences.

5. Standardization, Certification & Assessment Components: Implement international professional and managerial standards within the organization. Enable coordination with other organizations' quality systems and assess achievements against common scales (ISO 9000, CMM).

6. Human Components (Organizing for SQA): Include managers, testing personnel, the SQA unit, SQA trustees, committee members, and forum members. Their role is to initiate and support SQA implementation, detect deviations, and suggest improvements.

Q17
A system operates for 2000 hours with 8 failures. Total repair time is 16 hours. Calculate MTTF, MTTR, MTBF, Availability, and Failure Rate. Comment on system reliability.
5 marks

Given: Operating Time = 2000 hours, Failures = 8, Total Repair Time = 16 hours, Repairs = 8

MTTF = 2000 / 8 = 250 hours

MTTR = 16 / 8 = 2 hours

MTBF = MTTF + MTTR = 250 + 2 = 252 hours

Availability = 252 / (252 + 2) = 252 / 254 ≈ 0.9921 = 99.21%

Failure Rate (λ) = 8 / 2000 = 0.004 failures/hour

Comment on Reliability: An availability of 99.21% means the system is down approximately 0.79% of the time. For general business applications this may be acceptable, but for safety-critical systems (aircraft, medical devices) this is insufficient — they require ≥99.999% ("five nines") availability. To improve reliability, the organization should focus on defect prevention, better testing, and reducing repair time (MTTR).

Q18
Explain Statistical SQA techniques with the Pareto principle and Control Charts.
5 marks

Statistical SQA applies quantitative and statistical methods to measure and control software quality, enabling data-driven decision-making.

Pareto Analysis (80-20 Rule): Based on the observation that roughly 80% of defects are caused by 20% of the causes. In SQA, defects are categorized by type, and a Pareto chart displays them in descending order of frequency. Quality teams focus on the "vital few" causes responsible for the majority of defects. Example: if 65 of 100 bugs come from requirement and design errors, addressing these two categories eliminates most defects.

Control Charts (Statistical Process Control): Monitor process behavior over time. Three reference lines are plotted — the Center Line (CL, process average), Upper Control Limit (UCL), and Lower Control Limit (LCL). As long as process measurements fall within UCL and LCL, the process exhibits only common cause variation (acceptable). When a data point exceeds limits, it signals special cause variation (a problem) requiring investigation and corrective action.

Other Techniques: Histograms (visualize defect distribution), Scatter Diagrams (identify variable correlations), Run Charts (trend over time), and Fishbone/Ishikawa Diagrams (root cause analysis — heavily used at CMM Level 4).

Q19
Compare ISO 9001, ISO 9002, and ISO 9003. Which applies to software development and why?
5 marks

The ISO 9000 series includes three standards that apply to different types of organizations based on their activities:

ISO 9001: Applies to organizations engaged in design, development, production, and servicing of goods. This is the most comprehensive standard and directly applies to software development organizations because software development involves all four activities — requirements design, system architecture design, code production, and after-sale servicing/maintenance.

ISO 9002: Applies to organizations that only produce — they don't design products themselves but buy designs from external sources and manufacture. Examples: steel mills, car manufacturing plants that use externally sourced designs. ISO 9002 does not apply to software development organizations because they always design their own products.

ISO 9003: Applies to organizations involved only in installation and testing — not design or production. Example: gas supply companies. This has very limited applicability to software.

Conclusion: For software development organizations, ISO 9001 is the only applicable standard from this series. It is the standard organizations pursue for certification to demonstrate quality in their design, development, and service delivery activities.

Q20
What are Key Process Areas (KPAs)? Explain KPAs at CMM Levels 2, 3, 4, and 5.
5 marks

Key Process Areas (KPAs) are clusters of related activities that, when performed collectively, achieve a set of goals that improve process capability. Each CMM level (except Level 1) is defined by its KPAs. An organization must satisfy all KPAs at a given level before moving to the next.

Level 2 KPAs (Focus: Basic Management): Requirements Management, Software Project Planning, Software Project Tracking and Oversight, Software Subcontract Management, Software Quality Assurance, Software Configuration Management. Goal: establish disciplined tracking and basic management controls.

Level 3 KPAs (Focus: Organizational Standardization): Organization Process Focus, Organization Process Definition, Training Program, Integrated Software Management, Software Product Engineering, Intergroup Coordination, Peer Reviews. Goal: standardize processes organization-wide.

Level 4 KPAs (Focus: Quantitative Control): Quantitative Process Management, Software Quality Management. Goal: use metrics to predict and control process and product quality statistically.

Level 5 KPAs (Focus: Continuous Improvement): Defect Prevention, Technology Change Management, Process Change Management. Goal: institutionalize proactive improvement and innovation into everyday development practice.

Explain in detail the complete Formal Technical Review process — from planning to follow-up — for reviewing a critical banking transaction module. Include roles, meeting protocol, documentation, and decision outcomes.
10 marks

Context: The banking transaction module handles fund transfers. Errors here have direct financial consequences. FTR is applied to verify correctness and completeness before integration testing.

Step 1 — Review Scheduling and Planning: The Moderator schedules the FTR meeting at least 3 days in advance. The Author (transaction module developer) prepares and distributes the review package: source code, design documents, SRS section for transaction handling, and a specific review checklist covering boundary conditions, exception handling, concurrency, and security. Reviewers are given a maximum of 2 hours to prepare individually.

Step 2 — Participant Selection: Between 3–5 people are selected. For this module: the Author (transaction developer), a Senior Developer Reviewer (checks logic/algorithms), a Security Reviewer (checks auth and injection vulnerabilities), a Test Engineer (checks testability), and the Moderator (facilitates). The Manager ensures resources are available but does not attend in an evaluative role. The Recorder is assigned (often one of the reviewers).

Step 3 — Pre-Meeting Individual Review: Each reviewer studies the distributed material independently, marking issues, questions, and defects against the checklist. Issues are classified: Major (would cause functional failure), Minor (style, documentation, potential future issue), Query (clarification needed). The Moderator collects issue lists and prepares a consolidated agenda.

Step 4 — The Review Meeting (Max 2 Hours): Moderator opens by setting agenda and rules: review the product, not the developer; issues are logged, not solved; no defensive behavior. Author walks through the module section-by-section (not line-by-line). Reviewers raise pre-identified issues. The Recorder documents each issue with: issue ID, location in code, type (logic/security/etc.), severity (major/minor), and assigned owner. Moderator controls pace and prevents side debates. If an issue leads to extended technical debate, it is "parked" — noted, assigned to the Author for offline investigation, and not resolved during the meeting.

Step 5 — Meeting Outcome Decision: At the end of the meeting, all attendees decide collectively on one of three outcomes: (a) Accept: Module is functionally correct with no significant issues found — proceed to integration testing. (b) Conditional Accept: Minor issues found (e.g., missing null checks, unclear variable names) — Author corrects them; no second review needed; Moderator verifies corrections. (c) Reject: Major issues found (e.g., race condition in concurrent transactions, incorrect interest calculation formula) — Author must fix and the module must undergo a full re-review. All attendees sign a "sign-off sheet" indicating participation and agreement with findings.

Step 6 — Review Documentation: The Recorder prepares a Formal Technical Review Summary Report containing: list of attendees, work product reviewed, review date/duration, consolidated issue list with severities and owners, decision reached, and corrective action due dates. This report becomes part of the project's quality records and SQA audit trail.

Step 7 — Follow-Up: If Conditional Accept: Author corrects items within agreed deadline. Moderator performs a desk-check to confirm corrections. If Reject: Author performs corrections; new FTR is scheduled and the entire process repeats. Closure of all review issues is tracked in the defect management system.

Outcome for Banking Module: Typical FTR findings might include unchecked integer overflow in transaction amount calculations, missing rollback logic on failed transfers, or insufficient logging for audit trails. All critical findings discovered here prevent production incidents that would be far more expensive to fix post-deployment.

Current Availability = 98.70% = 0.9870

Q25
Critically compare ISO 9000 and SEI-CMM as quality frameworks. When should an organization choose one over the other? Use examples.
10 marks

Both ISO 9000 and SEI-CMM are quality improvement frameworks, but they differ fundamentally in scope, approach, and applicability.

ISO 9000 — Overview: An international standard (declared 1987) developed by a consortium of 63 countries. It provides a requirements-based framework for establishing a quality management system. ISO 9001 (the certifiable standard) defines what an organization must do to ensure quality. Certification is a binary result — either certified or not. The focus is on documentation, process definition, and customer satisfaction across any industry.

SEI-CMM — Overview: Developed specifically for software by Carnegie Mellon's SEI. It provides a staged, evolutionary roadmap from Level 1 (chaotic) to Level 5 (optimizing). Rather than binary certification, CMM provides a continuous improvement scale. It is prescriptive about how to improve software processes through specific Key Process Areas.

Key Differences:

Scope: ISO 9000 applies to any industry (manufacturing, service, software). CMM is software-specific. A bakery can get ISO 9001; only a software organization pursues CMM.

Assessment: ISO 9000 results in a pass/fail certification. CMM produces a maturity level rating (1–5), giving a much richer picture of organizational capability and improvement trajectory.

Prescription: ISO 9001 defines what to achieve (document processes, meet customer requirements, continually improve) but not how. CMM prescribes specific KPAs and practices to follow at each level, making it more actionable for software organizations.

Continuity: ISO 9001 certification requires periodic re-audits to maintain. CMM improvement is continuous and does not expire — but typically organizations seek periodic re-assessments for benchmarking.

Level Correspondence: ISO 9001 certification roughly corresponds to CMM Level 3 (Defined) — where processes are documented and standardized. CMM Levels 4 and 5 go significantly beyond ISO 9001 in quantitative measurement and continuous innovation.

When to Choose ISO 9000: (1) A software company that also serves non-software clients or operates in regulated industries where ISO certification is contractually required. (2) When entering international markets where ISO certification is a procurement requirement. (3) When the organization wants a universally recognized quality badge that applies across departments (HR, procurement, development). Example: a software company bidding for a government supply contract where ISO 9001 is mandatory.

When to Choose CMM: (1) When the organization is purely software-focused and wants a detailed, actionable roadmap for process improvement. (2) When bidding for US DoD contracts or other defense/aerospace contracts where CMM Level 3 or higher is required. (3) When the organization wants to mature from Level 1 chaos to disciplined, metric-driven development. Example: an Indian IT services company (like Infosys or TCS) seeking CMMI Level 5 to demonstrate world-class software processes to multinational clients.

Best Practice: Leading organizations pursue both. ISO 9001 provides the external, universally recognized certification. CMM provides the internal improvement roadmap. An organization achieves ISO 9001 at CMM Level 3 and continues CMM improvement toward Levels 4 and 5 beyond ISO requirements.

Q26
Explain all five software reliability models. Under what conditions would you recommend Goel-Okumoto over Jelinski-Moranda? Use a comparative scenario.
8 marks

1. Jelinski–Moranda Model: This is the oldest and simplest reliability model. It assumes that the software contains N initial defects, each contributing an equal and constant hazard (failure rate) to the overall system. When a defect is discovered and removed, the failure rate drops by exactly 1/N. Assumption: all defects are equally likely to be triggered and equally simple to fix. Limitation: unrealistic in practice — some defects are much harder to find and some contribute far more to failures than others.

2. Goel–Okumoto (NHPP) Model: Uses a Non-Homogeneous Poisson Process. The key assumption is that the failure rate changes over time (not constant). The expected number of failures by time t follows a concave, S-shaped or exponential growth curve. More flexible than Jelinski–Moranda because it models the reality that defect detection rates vary across testing phases. Widely used in industry for predicting cumulative failures over time.

3. Musa's Basic Execution Time Model: Instead of calendar time, this model uses CPU execution time as the basis for measurement. Failure rate is proportional to execution intensity (how actively the program is used). The model links reliability to actual usage — a program that runs rarely will show fewer failures than one running 24/7. Very practical for embedded and mission-critical software.

4. Schneidewind Model: Divides the testing period into intervals and applies least squares regression to observed failure data to predict future failure counts. It is particularly useful when you have historical failure data from multiple testing phases and want to forecast remaining defects.

5. Littlewood–Verrall Model: Assumes a variable failure rate per defect — unlike Jelinski–Moranda's constant rate. Each defect has its own difficulty level, so some defects trigger failures more frequently than others. This is more realistic and statistically sound, though more complex to parameterize.

Jelinski–Moranda vs. Goel–Okumoto — Comparison:

Scenario: You are testing an e-commerce platform. In the first week, failures occur frequently (10/day). In the third week, failures drop to 2/day. In week 5, only 0.3/day. This is a classic decreasing failure rate pattern — exactly what NHPP models like Goel–Okumoto capture elegantly. Jelinski–Moranda would predict a linear, uniform decrease (proportional to defects removed), which doesn't match this real-world S-curve behavior.

Recommendation: Use Goel–Okumoto when: the failure rate is non-constant (varies over testing phases), you have enough historical data to fit the NHPP curve, and you need to predict the remaining number of failures and optimal software release time. Use Jelinski–Moranda only for quick, rough estimates when minimal data is available and uniform defect characteristics can be assumed.

Q27
Define and differentiate: Quality Control vs. Quality Assurance vs. Total Quality Management. Provide a real-world software example for each.
5 marks

Quality Control (QC): QC is a reactive process that identifies and removes defects from a finished or near-finished product. It focuses on product inspection and defect detection. Additionally, QC determines the causes of defects so they can be corrected — not just rejected. It is a step up from pure inspection. Software Example: Running a comprehensive regression test suite against build v2.1 and finding 15 bugs. Fixing those bugs and re-testing. The testing activity itself is QC.

Quality Assurance (QA): QA is proactive and process-focused. The premise is: if the development process is properly defined and followed, quality products will naturally result. QA defines the processes, standards, and procedures that the development team must follow — before and during development, not just at the end. Software Example: Defining a mandatory code review checklist, a testing policy, and a change management procedure. Auditing projects to confirm these policies are followed. ISO 9000 embodies QA philosophy.

Total Quality Management (TQM): TQM takes QA further by requiring continuous process improvement through measurement and optimization. It doesn't just document good processes — it actively optimizes them using metrics and data. Linked to Business Process Reengineering (BPR). Involves all employees in quality improvement. Software Example: Using monthly Pareto analysis of defect data to identify that requirement-phase errors cause 60% of all bugs. Redesigning the requirements workshop process to include structured review and user story validation. Measuring the improvement. Then repeating the cycle. This is TQM in software.

Q28
What are the types of software reviews? Compare Walkthrough, Technical Review, and Inspection on the basis of purpose, rigor, participants, and documentation.
5 marks

Software reviews are systematic inspections of software work products to detect defects early in the SDLC. The three main peer review types differ significantly in structure and purpose:

Walkthrough: An author-led review where the author guides team members through the software artifact (design, code, or document). The primary purpose is educational — team members learn the author's approach while also providing defect feedback. Participants ask questions and comment on defects. Rigor is low to moderate — no strict agenda is required. Documentation is informal. Best used early in development for knowledge transfer and high-level issue discovery.

Technical Review: A team of highly qualified individuals (domain experts, architects, security specialists) examines the software against client use cases and established technical standards. Purpose is to identify technical defects from a standards/compliance perspective. Rigor is high. Participants are selected for specific technical expertise. Documentation includes a formal technical issues list. Best used for critical architectural decisions, security review, or standards compliance verification.

Inspection: The most rigorous and formal review type. Reviewers follow a well-defined, documented inspection process with specific checklists, entry/exit criteria, and defined roles (Moderator, Author, Reviewer, Recorder). Purpose is systematic, disciplined defect detection. Documentation is comprehensive and becomes part of the project quality record. Historically shown to find the highest defect density of any review technique. Best used for critical modules before integration testing.

Summary: Walkthrough (low formality, educational) → Technical Review (medium-high formality, expertise-driven) → Inspection (highest formality, defect-focused). FTR encompasses all three, with Inspection being the FTR ideal.

Q29
SCENARIO: Two students debate CMM. Student A says: "CMM Level 3 is sufficient — once processes are documented, we're done." Student B says: "Level 3 is just the beginning — Levels 4 and 5 deliver the real competitive advantage." Who is correct? Justify with technical detail and industry examples.
10 marks

Position: Student B is correct. CMM Level 3, while representing a significant achievement, is foundational — not the destination. Levels 4 and 5 deliver qualitatively different and measurably superior outcomes.

Why Level 3 is "Just the Beginning":

At CMM Level 3 (Defined), processes are documented, standardized, and consistently followed. This eliminates the chaos of Level 1 and the management gaps of Level 2. However, Level 3 has a critical weakness: process and product quality are not quantitatively measured. Engineers know what process to follow but cannot answer: "Is our process working well? By how much did our defect rate improve? Will this release meet our quality targets?" At Level 3, decision-making is still largely intuition-based, not data-driven. This is exactly what ISO 9000 achieves — and while ISO 9001 is valuable, world-class software organizations don't stop there.

CMM Level 4 — The Power of Measurement: At Level 4 (Managed), the organization deploys comprehensive software metrics. Product metrics (defect density, code complexity, reliability) and Process metrics (defect removal efficiency, productivity per phase, review effectiveness) are collected and analyzed. This enables true process control: if the defect rate in code reviews exceeds the Upper Control Limit on a control chart, engineers know immediately and investigate the cause. Decisions are evidence-based, not opinion-based. Example: a Level 4 organization can calculate their Defect Removal Efficiency (DRE) — if they find 90% of all defects before release, they know 10% reach customers and can calculate expected field failure rates. They can make a quantitative release decision: "Based on current DRE and defect density, release will have ≤0.3 defects/KLOC." A Level 3 organization simply cannot make this statement.

CMM Level 5 — The Innovation Edge: At Level 5 (Optimizing), the organization uses collected measurement data to drive continuous process improvement and defect prevention. Rather than reacting to defects, they analyze root causes of defect classes and redesign the process to prevent the entire class from occurring. Technology Change Management means new tools (AI-assisted code review, static analysis) are adopted in a managed way that enhances quality. Process Change Management ensures improvements propagate organization-wide. This is the competitive differentiator in the global software market.

Industry Examples: Companies like NASA's Software Engineering Division achieved CMM Level 5 and reported defect densities of 0.1 defects per KLOC — compared to industry averages of 1–10 defects/KLOC. Indian IT giants like TCS, Infosys, and Wipro have maintained CMMI Level 5 (the modern evolution of CMM) because their enterprise clients — Boeing, Goldman Sachs, healthcare providers — contractually require it as proof of process maturity. These organizations don't just document processes (Level 3) — they measure, analyze, and optimize them continuously.

To Student A: Documentation is necessary but not sufficient. Documented processes that aren't measured can silently degrade. Without Level 4 measurement, you don't know if your Level 3 processes are actually effective. Without Level 5 innovation, you don't improve them. Level 3 is the foundation; Levels 4 and 5 build the competitive advantage.

📦 Concept Summary Cards

Software Quality
Fitness of Purpose — does what users want
Attributes: Correctness, Reliability, Efficiency, Integrity, Usability, Maintainability, Portability, Reusability, Flexibility
McCall's Model: Operation + Revision + Transition
Functional correctness ≠ Quality (bad UI example)
QMS Evolution (4 Stages)
1. Inspection — find & remove defective products
2. QC — find defects + root cause analysis
3. QA — good process → good product (ISO 9000)
4. TQM — continuous improvement + BPR
Shift: Product Assurance → Process Assurance
FTR Quick Facts
Participants: 3–5 people
Prep: ≤2 hours per person
Duration: <2 hours
Roles: Moderator, Author, Reviewer, Recorder, Manager
Outcomes: Accept / Conditional Accept / Reject
Review the PRODUCT, not the PRODUCER
CMM 5 Levels
L1 Initial — Chaotic, ad hoc (no KPAs)
L2 Repeatable — Basic PM, COCOMO, FP estimation
L3 Defined — Documented org-wide; ISO 9000 level
L4 Managed — Metrics, Pareto, control charts
L5 Optimizing — Continuous improvement, defect prevention
ISO 9000 Key Points
9001 — Design+Dev+Production+Service (software ✅)
9002 — Production only (no software design ❌)
9003 — Installation & testing only ❌
7 Principles: Customer Focus, Leadership, People, Process, Improvement, Evidence, Relationships
Steps: Apply → Pre-assess → Doc Review → Compliance → Register → Continued inspection
SQA System
Ongoing process — quality in EVERY phase
6 Component Classes: Pre-project, Lifecycle Assessment, Error Prevention, Quality Mgmt, Standardization, Human
Pre-project: Contract Review + Quality Plan
SQA Plan: Management, Standards, Reviews, Testing, Config Mgmt
Statistical SQA
Pareto (80-20): 20% causes → 80% defects
Control Charts: UCL / CL / LCL — detect special cause variation
Histogram: visualize defect distribution
Scatter Diagram: correlation between variables
Fishbone: root cause analysis (Level 4)
Review Types Comparison
Walkthrough: Author-led, educational, informal
Technical Review: Expert-led, standards compliance
Inspection: Most rigorous, defined process, checklists
Code Review: Source code examination
Pair Programming: 2 devs, 1 workstation, simultaneous
Audit: External reviewers, compliance check
Reliability Models (5)
Jelinski–Moranda: Constant failure rate / defect
Goel–Okumoto: NHPP, variable rate over time ⭐
Musa: Based on CPU execution time
Schneidewind: Least squares, interval-based
Littlewood–Verrall: Variable rate per defect (most realistic)
